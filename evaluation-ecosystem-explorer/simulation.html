How the simulation works

We want to know how an evaluation ecosystem responds when one part of its design changes: what providers build once a benchmark is hidden, or which feedback channels dampen gaming and which amplify it. Questions like these are hard to study directly. Case studies document outcomes after they appear, and the real ecosystem cannot be rerun with one design choice changed. Game-theoretic models become intractable once actors differ from each other, see different information, and interact through many feedback channels. System-dynamics models keep the feedback loops but average the actors away. A simulation gives up realism for control: it does not predict what real evaluation markets will do, but it lets one lever change while everything else holds fixed. Ours is a generative agent-based model, a recent approach in which the strategic actors are LLMs that read the same kind of material real decision-makers do (incident reports, competitor moves, regulatory news) and act on it, while the market itself runs on explicit rules (Park et al. 2023; Vezhnevets et al. 2023). We treat the simulation as a complement to those other methods and as a source of hypotheses for empirical work to test, not as evidence about deployed systems; the final section states the boundary of the results and grades every structural assumption by the evidence behind it.

The simulation runs a stylized AI evaluation ecosystem for 40 monthly rounds. Six competing providers build models, a single evaluator scores them and publishes a leaderboard, 51 consumer segments adopt and switch, a regulator intervenes, four funder types allocate capital, and a media actor decides what gets covered. Providers, the regulator, and funders reason with an LLM; media and the consumer market are rule-based.

How a score and a user's experience come apart

Real models are compared on many axes at once: reasoning, coding, knowledge, safety, communication, and agentic work. The simulation takes that list literally and treats a model as a point in a six-dimensional space, one axis per capability. A benchmark score is a weighted average of those six numbers, with the weights set by what the benchmark tests. A user's experience is a different weighted average, with the weights set by what that user needs. Because the weights differ, the same improvement to a model can raise the score a lot and the experience very little, or the reverse. The abstraction is coarse, and it does one job: it keeps what is measured separate from what is needed.

In this model, gaming is the gap between those two averages. A provider can see the benchmark's weights, or at least infer them, but not its users'. When it directs research toward the target it can see, the gap opens.

In the figure below, a provider has a fixed research budget and one choice: aim it at the dimensions the benchmark rewards, or at the dimensions its audience needs. During a real run the provider sees neither target exactly; it infers the benchmark's weights from noisy scores and gets only a rough signal of what its users need. Here both targets are fixed and visible, so you can aim by hand and watch the score and the satisfaction readout respond.

Six capability dimensions, 51 consumer segments

Benchmarks, consumer needs, and provider capabilities are all described by weights over the same six dimensions. Sharing one space is what lets the score and the user's experience in Section 1 be compared at all. It is also a simplification: real capability has far more than six axes.

What happens in one round

One round is one month. Actors act in a fixed order, so each sees the consequences of those before it. Capital and regulatory decisions take effect at the start of the next round, which closes the loop.

Who knows what

Information in the simulation sits in three tiers, and the code enforces the split. Public state is visible to everyone. Private state is visible only to its owner. Ground truth, which includes the true capability vectors, the benchmarks' true weights, and true consumer satisfaction, is held by the simulation itself: no actor can read it, and it never appears in an LLM prompt. The gap in Section 1 is measured against that hidden tier.

Interactive · what each actor sees
Score reliability is computed after the run

Score reliability, the correlation between published scores and the satisfaction consumers actually got, is never computed during a run. The simulation logs the ingredients every round and researchers compute it afterwards from the log. No actor receives it. Actors can only notice that scores are misleading through indirect signs: incidents, drifting market share, media coverage.

How providers learn what a benchmark rewards

A provider cannot see which dimensions a benchmark weights. It sees only its own score. Each round it predicts the score its current capabilities should earn, compares the prediction with the score it got, and moves its guess about the weights in the direction of the error. The step size is 0.15 for most providers and 0.10 for the two safety-focused ones. The same rule runs whether a provider plans with an LLM or with heuristics: it updates what the provider believes about the benchmark, and the provider then decides what to do with that belief.

The case study: private holdouts redistribute the gap

The obvious response to benchmark gaming is to stop showing the test. Hold items back, publish less often, and the provider can no longer aim precisely. Making a benchmark private changes three things at once, and they do not all push the same way.

Each condition sets two of these together: how much of each benchmark is hidden, and how far the hidden weights sit from the public ones.

The finding

Moving from public benchmarks to private holdouts redistributes, rather than closes, the gap between benchmark scores and user experience. Aiming gets harder, but what benchmarks reward and what users need still differ, so the gap reappears elsewhere in the roster. Holdout design changes where the gap sits, not whether there is one.

Other questions the simulation can ask

Implemented means the condition exists and can be run; designed means it is specified but not yet built. None of these is a finding yet.

implementedDo EU-style precautionary and US-style light-touch regulatory regimes push safety investment and market structure in different directions?
designedWhat does a mandate to disclose safety investment, in the style of California SB 53, change for consumers, funders, and media?
designedWhat happens when informal media signals, rather than formal benchmarks, become the primary channel consumers listen to?

Claims and limitations

The simulation generates hypotheses. It points at dynamics worth checking empirically; it does not show that they occur in real systems. Its claims come in two kinds. The numbers, such as how large a gap gets or how fast it grows, belong to this particular parameterization and should not be carried anywhere else. The mechanisms, meaning which direction a design change pushes outcomes and why, are the findings.

The boundary

The results apply to settings where public benchmarks and leaderboards drive decisions and consumers need different things. To judge whether they carry to a new setting, say a domain evaluated entirely through internal test suites, ask which of the four conditions below it weakens.

Four conditions the central mechanism needs:

Watched scoresPublished scores steer some real decisions: consumer choices, coverage, capital. If nobody acts on the leaderboard, there is no pressure to optimize against it.
Asymmetric investmentAt least one capability dimension has a dedicated investment pathway and at least one does not. With a single dimension, hiding a benchmark's weights would add nothing but noise to the score.
Heterogeneous needsConsumers weight capabilities differently from each other and from benchmarks. If every consumer needed the same thing, the central finding would vanish by construction.
Adaptive providersProviders use score feedback to adjust what they build. A minimal error-correction rule is enough.

Underneath those sit seven structural assumptions, each graded by the evidence behind it: calibrated means fit to external data, anchored means published studies support the qualitative shape, and stipulated means a modeling choice with a rationale but no direct empirical support.

The weakest link is the satisfaction function itself, which is stipulated: it has not been validated against what users report wanting, and every gap number inherits that uncertainty. Measuring the gap against different consumer groups changes its size substantially but has never changed the direction of a result.

Some things are left out on purpose. The list is not exhaustive; it covers the omissions most likely to change how evaluation itself behaves.

Competing evaluatorsA single evaluator defines the roster and every benchmark's weights. There is no second leaderboard weighting the same axis differently, and no standards body reconciling the two, so a provider never chooses which measurement to answer to.
PriceCost is a fixed per-provider constant weighted by a fixed consumer sensitivity. No provider cuts price, and nobody trades capability against cost per token, so the leaderboard carries competitive weight that price and latency absorb in practice.
Internal evaluation suitesProviders are measured only by the public evaluator. No internal test suite gates a release and no live-traffic result feeds back into what gets built, so the leaderboard is the only measurement pressure on R&D.
Multi-model usersAdoption is a share of a segment, and each switching decision is made from the position of one incumbent. Real usage is closer to a continuous split of traffic across several models at once, routed by task, with a second kept as a fallback.
Distribution advantageEvery provider reaches every consumer through the same channel. Bundling a model into a product people already use is not represented, so a weaker model cannot win on placement.
Startup entryNo provider is created mid-run, so the roster of six is fixed. A new lab has no track record and no installed base, which leaves a public benchmark result as the main credible claim it can make, and open-source labs lean on this hardest by publishing weights and scores in place of a commercial reputation. Holding entry out understates how much pressure the leaderboard is under.
Shifting needsConsumer need weights are fixed for the run, so growing demand for agentic capability over time is not modeled.

In most runs every LLM actor reasons with the same underlying model, so when strategies converge, part of that may be the model's own habits rather than the incentives. Robustness across models is checked separately.
