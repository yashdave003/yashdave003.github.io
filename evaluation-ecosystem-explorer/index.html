The frame

Benchmarks do more than measure. Once one gains traction, its influence runs in both directions: the same scores that signal progress also reshape how providers allocate research effort, where funders direct capital, and which failure modes regulators attend to. Yet a benchmark is usually judged as a one-shot instrument: calibration, validity, contamination resistance, with the world around it held fixed. This work reframes the question from “is this metric well-calibrated?” to “what does the ecosystem do with this benchmark once it exists?” Our simulation models that ecosystem as six stylized actors (providers, evaluators, consumers, regulators, funders, and media) linked by feedback loops through development, the market, and regulation.

The six actors are a simplification. The grey boxes on the ring are real actor types the model leaves out: colored means modeled, grey means outside the modeled core. Click any box to see who's inside.

About the simulation

The simulation combines rule-based market dynamics with LLM-driven strategic actors, a form of generative agent-based modeling. Benchmarks, consumer needs, and provider capabilities are all described by weights over six capability dimensions (reasoning, coding, knowledge, safety, communication, agentic). Information is split into tiers: every actor sees public state and holds its own private state (shown when you click its box), while ground truth, meaning true capabilities, true consumer satisfaction, and the benchmarks' true weights, is held by the simulation and hidden from every actor. The gap between what scores say and what users experience is measured against that hidden tier. As a case study, the paper applies the simulation to benchmark holdout design and finds that private holdout benchmarks redistribute, rather than close, the gap between benchmark scores and user experience.

The simulation generates hypotheses; it is not evidence that these dynamics occur in deployed systems. Its results are claimed only for settings where public benchmarks and leaderboards drive decisions and consumers need different things. The explainer's final section states that boundary, grades each structural assumption by the evidence behind it, and lists what is left out on purpose.

Beyond the six actors

The grey boxes make one point: the real ecosystem is much larger, and the six actors do not divide it cleanly. Real organizations sit at intersections: a government safety institute is both an evaluator and an arm of regulation; an arena leaderboard began as academic research and became a venture-backed company; a nonprofit institute funds research grants, co-develops a benchmark, and maintains the harness it runs on. Clicking any box opens its roster; names with a dotted underline play multiple roles, and hovering one traces it across the map.

This ecosystem frame is also distinct from supply-chain and value-chain framings, two neighboring views that deserve their own maps. The AI supply chain (Hopkins et al. 2025) traces how models, datasets, and services compose into products, the chain the EU AI Act regulates as the “AI value chain” (Article 25). And beneath all of it sits compute and infrastructure (semiconductors, data centers, cloud, energy), studied as compute governance (Sastry et al. 2024) and mapped by industry as “the AI value chain.” Neither is modeled in our simulation; both shape what gets built and what gets evaluated.
